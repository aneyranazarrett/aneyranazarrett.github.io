Research

I am interested in the intersection between business and government: how policies shape firm behavior, and how firms guide and influence policy.

Job Market Paper

  • Managers, Wage Premia, and Worker Effort: Evidence from a Large Retailer
    Job Market Paper
    Latest version (PDF)

    I study managerial monitoring and wage premia as levers to incentivize worker effort in the firm. Using administrative records from a large retailer, I show how the two jointly deter inventory shrinkage, merchandise missing from the store. Exploiting the rotation of managers across stores, I estimate each manager's effect on shrinkage and document what high-type managers do when they arrive at a store. They are not better bookkeepers. They work more shifts, including nights and weekends, spend more time on the floor with their workers, and rework the teams they inherit, yet on demographic characteristics they are indistinguishable from other managers. Leveraging a company policy that sets wages centrally by position, I document that shrinkage also falls with the wage premium. The two levers reinforce each other: the arrival of a good manager reduces shrinkage by 8 percent more where the premium is high. Good managers suspend more workers during high-wage-premium periods.

Working Papers

In my research I study what makes firms productive.

  • Training Within Firms

    with Brayan Diaz, Julian Ramirez, Raffaella Sadun, and Jorge Tamayo  ·  NBER Working Paper No. 33670 · HBS Working Paper 25-045

    Training investments are essential for improving worker and firm productivity, yet their implementation is often hindered by low participation rates and insufficient worker engagement. This study uses data from three firms (a car manufacturer, a quick-service restaurant chain, and a retail company) to show that variation in training participation among employees is closely tied to differences in middle managers’ behavior and practices. Middle managers who actively engage with their employees and emphasize their well-being and development are associated with significantly higher participation in training programs. These managerial differences significantly influence employee performance and absenteeism, especially during periods of organizational change. Together, these findings underscore the importance of middle managers in bridging the gap between centrally designed HR policies and their effective on-the-ground execution.

    Presented at the NBER Summer Institute (SI 2024, Personnel Economics).

  • What Do Good Managers Do and What Do They Leave Behind?

    with Achyuta Adhvaryu, Parker Howell, Anant Nyshadham, and Jorge Tamayo

    Presented at the NBER Summer Institute (SI 2025, Personnel Economics).

    Using administrative data from a large Colombian retailer and exploiting a manager rotation policy to estimate manager value-added, we study what good managers do and whether their impacts persist after they leave. Good managers increase sales by 12%, reducing stockouts while making inventory leaner, generating larger revenue gains from price changes without increasing the frequency of repricing, and shifting personnel attention toward back-office operations. When they depart, the staffing and inventory improvements persist, but pricing-related gains erode. These patterns reveal a fundamental distinction: some managerial contributions are embedded in organizational structure, while others depend on the manager’s ongoing, context-specific judgment.

  • VAT Holidays as Short-Term Economic Stimuli: Evidence from Colombia’s “Días sin IVA” during COVID-19

    with José Martinez-Carrasco, David Muñoz-Sanchez, Alejandro Rasteletti, and Diego Zamora  ·  IDB Working Paper

    Sales tax holidays (STH) are commonly used to spur short-term consumption, yet the evidence on their effectiveness in developing countries remains limited. This paper analyzes Colombia’s Días sin IVA (DSI), a nationwide VAT holiday held eight times between 2020 to 2022. The policy temporarily cut the 19% VAT to 0% on selected goods, covering about 40% of formal household consumption. Using high-frequency transaction data and a repeated regression discontinuity design, we find that DSI increased average sales in formal businesses by 15%, after accounting for intertemporal substitution. Unlike previous studies, we show evidence of substantial spillover effects across sectors. While businesses selling goods subject to the VAT reduction experienced a 17% average increase in sales, those selling goods with non-reduced VAT increased their sales by 14%, with widespread heterogeneous effects across diverse product categories.

  • Trade Barriers Without Tariffs: Border Tightening and Pass-Through in Retail Markets

    Solo-authored  ·  Work in progress

    Do border-induced logistics disruptions spill over into domestic retail markets? When U.S. authorities tighten truck inspections at crossings such as Laredo and El Paso, freight stalls for a day or more, and many Mexican suppliers serve both U.S. export markets and domestic supermarkets. I study what happens on Mexican shelves using scanner data from a large supermarket chain in Mexico: prices, quantities, stockouts, and purchase orders linked to supplier IDs, which lets me identify which suppliers export to the United States and which border crossings they use. An event-study design compares products from exporting suppliers with similar products in the same category from suppliers that do not export, and, within exporters, those that rely on the affected crossings with those that use unaffected ones. The outcomes are temporary unavailability, delivery timing, prices, and product quality, proxied by the share of perishables arriving unsellable. The design separates a short-run effect, in which goods that cannot cross are redirected to local shelves, from the longer-run strain that repeated frictions place on supply.

  • AI Tools for Store Managers: Training, Adaptation, and Store Performance

    Field experiment with a large retailer in Mexico  ·  In preparation

    How do store managers put AI to work, and what changes in their stores when they do? In a field experiment with a large retailer in Mexico, I train store managers to use AI tools in two ways. In one arm, managers receive general training in using AI, with no instruction on how to apply it to specific tasks, and I observe which tasks they adapt it to on their own. In the other, managers are trained on specific tasks that can be done with AI, and I measure the impact on store performance. Comparing the two arms shows whether the gains from AI in the store come from the tool itself or from telling managers what to do with it.
